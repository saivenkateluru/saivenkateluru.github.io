## 1. A recurrent model for sequences

Let the input be a sequence $x_{1:T}=(x_1,\ldots,x_T)$ with $x_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d_x}$. A vanilla recurrent neural network maintains a hidden state $h_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d_h}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_t &= W_hh_{t-1}+W_xx_t+b_h,\\
h_t &= \phi(z_t),\\
o_t &= W_oh_t+b_o,\\
p_t &= \operatorname{softmax}(o_t).
\end{aligned}
$$

The same parameters are reused at every time step. Thus $h_t$ is not merely a feature vector for $x_t$; recursively, it is a learned summary of the complete prefix $x_{1:t}$.

<figure className="source-figure">
  <img src="/blog/from-recurrent-memory-to-attention/rnn-unrolled-notes.png" alt="Lecture notes showing a recurrent neural network unrolled through time" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 1.</strong> An RNN unrolled through time. Every green cell represents the same transition function with shared parameters. Adapted from Prof. Prathosh A. P.'s lecture notes.</figcaption>
</figure>

For token-level supervision, take

$$
L=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_t,
\qquad
\ell_t=-\log p_t[y_t].
$$

Although the forward equations are short, two constraints are already visible:

1. **Sequential computation:** $h_t$ cannot be computed before $h_{t-1}$.
2. **Repeated transport:** information from time $t-k$ must cross $k$ recurrent transitions to affect time $t$.

The second point becomes precise only after we backpropagate.

## 2. Backpropagation through time

Consider a perturbation $dh_{t-1}$. Since

$$
z_t=W_hh_{t-1}+W_xx_t+b_h,
$$

we have $dz_t=W_h\,dh_{t-1}$. Applying the elementwise nonlinearity gives

$$
dh_t=D_t\,dz_t,
\qquad
D_t=\operatorname{Diag}\!\left(\phi'(z_t)\right).
$$

Therefore the one-step hidden-state Jacobian is

$$
\boxed{
J_t
=\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-1}}
=D_tW_h.
}
$$

This is where the Jacobian enters: it is simply the local linear map carrying a small change in $h_{t-1}$ to the resulting change in $h_t$.

### Crossing several time steps

Repeated use of the chain rule gives

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-k}}
=J_tJ_{t-1}\cdots J_{t-k+1}.
}
$$

For column-vector gradients, a loss at time $t$ sends the signal backward as

$$
\nabla_{h_{t-k}}\ell_t
=\left(J_tJ_{t-1}\cdots J_{t-k+1}\right)^\top
\nabla_{h_t}\ell_t.
$$

<details className="doubt">
  <summary>Why does the transpose appear in the backward equation?</summary>
  <p>The forward differential is $dh_t=J_tdh_{t-1}$. Matching $d\ell=(\nabla_{h_t}\ell)^\top dh_t$ with $d\ell=(\nabla_{h_{t-1}}\ell)^\top dh_{t-1}$ gives $\nabla_{h_{t-1}}\ell=J_t^\top\nabla_{h_t}\ell$.</p>
</details>

<figure className="source-figure source-figure--portrait">
  <img src="/blog/from-recurrent-memory-to-attention/bptt-jacobian-notes.png" alt="Lecture derivation of the recurrent Jacobian product in backpropagation through time" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 2.</strong> The chain-rule expansion that produces the product of recurrent Jacobians. Adapted from Prof. Prathosh A. P.'s lecture notes.</figcaption>
</figure>

The complete sequence loss contributes at every later time. A convenient backward recursion is

$$
g_t
=\nabla_{h_t}L
=\nabla_{h_t}\ell_t+J_{t+1}^{\top}g_{t+1},
$$

with $g_T=\nabla_{h_T}\ell_T$. Defining

$$
\delta_t=\nabla_{z_t}L=D_tg_t,
$$

the gradient of the shared recurrent matrix accumulates contributions from all its uses:

$$
\boxed{
\nabla_{W_h}L
=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\delta_t h_{t-1}^{\top}.
}
$$

This procedure is called **backpropagation through time (BPTT)**: unroll the shared recurrence as a depth-$T$ computation graph, then apply ordinary backpropagation.

## 3. Why gradients vanish or explode

Take spectral norms in the long-range gradient equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left\|\nabla_{h_{t-k}}\ell_t\right\|_2
&\le
\left(\prod_{j=t-k+1}^{t}\|J_j\|_2\right)
\left\|\nabla_{h_t}\ell_t\right\|_2\\
&\le
\left(\prod_{j=t-k+1}^{t}\|D_j\|_2\|W_h\|_2\right)
\left\|\nabla_{h_t}\ell_t\right\|_2.
\end{aligned}
$$

If every factor satisfies $\|J_j\|_2\le\gamma<1$, then

$$
\left\|\nabla_{h_{t-k}}\ell_t\right\|_2
\le \gamma^k\left\|\nabla_{h_t}\ell_t\right\|_2,
$$

which decays exponentially with the temporal distance $k$. Saturating nonlinearities make this particularly plausible: for $\phi=\tanh$, entries of $D_j$ approach zero when $|z_j|$ is large.

If the product repeatedly expands vectors instead, the gradient can grow exponentially. The optimizer then receives huge, unstable updates; gradient clipping can control their magnitude, but it cannot recreate a signal that has already vanished.

<details className="doubt">
  <summary>Does $\|J_j\|_2&gt;1$ at every step guarantee an exploding gradient?</summary>
  <p>No. The norm bound describes the largest possible expansion. Actual explosion also requires the transported gradient to remain aligned with expanding singular directions across many changing Jacobians.</p>
</details>

<figure className="source-figure source-figure--portrait">
  <img src="/blog/from-recurrent-memory-to-attention/gradient-failure-notes.png" alt="Lecture notes explaining vanishing and exploding recurrent gradients using a product of matrix norms" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 3.</strong> Long-range credit assignment is controlled by a product of local Jacobians. Adapted from Prof. Prathosh A. P.'s lecture notes.</figcaption>
</figure>

<div className="takeaway">The problem is not recurrence by itself. It is forcing every long-range gradient through a long product of dense, nonlinear state-transition Jacobians.</div>

## 4. Gated additive memory paths

LSTMs and GRUs do not make the sequence parallel. Their intervention is different: construct an additive path along which information and gradients can be copied rather than repeatedly rewritten.

### LSTM: a separate cell-state highway

One common LSTM parameterization is

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_t &= \sigma(W_fx_t+U_fh_{t-1}+b_f),\\
i_t &= \sigma(W_ix_t+U_ih_{t-1}+b_i),\\
\widetilde c_t &= \tanh(W_cx_t+U_ch_{t-1}+b_c),\\
c_t &= f_t\odot c_{t-1}+i_t\odot\widetilde c_t,\\
o_t &= \sigma(W_ox_t+U_oh_{t-1}+b_o),\\
h_t &= o_t\odot\tanh(c_t).
\end{aligned}
$$

The forget gate $f_t$ controls retention, the input gate $i_t$ controls writing, and the output gate $o_t$ controls exposure.

<figure className="source-figure">
  <img src="/blog/from-recurrent-memory-to-attention/lstm-gates-lecture.png" alt="Lecture slide showing the forget, input, and output gates of an LSTM" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 4.</strong> The three decisions made by an LSTM cell: retain, write, and expose. From Prof. Prathosh A. P.'s sequence-model lecture.</figcaption>
</figure>

Now isolate the direct edge $c_{j-1}\rightarrow c_j$ in the computation graph. Holding the gates fixed along this edge,

$$
F_j
:=
\left.\frac{\partial c_j}{\partial c_{j-1}}\right|_{\text{direct cell edge}}
=\operatorname{Diag}(f_j).
$$

Hence a gradient travelling only along the cell-state chain obeys

$$
\left.\nabla_{c_{t-k}}L\right|_{\text{cell path}}
=
\left(F_tF_{t-1}\cdots F_{t-k+1}\right)^\top
\nabla_{c_t}L.
$$

This already shows the change from a vanilla RNN: the repeated factor is a learned diagonal retention gate, not $D_jW_h$.

### From $h_{t-k}$ all the way to $h_t$

To connect this result to the hidden states, isolate the computational path

$$
h_{t-k}longrightarrow c_{t-k+1}longrightarrow c_{t-k+2}longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow c_t\longrightarrow h_t.
$$

Let

$$
G_{t-k+1}
=\frac{\partial c_{t-k+1}}{\partial h_{t-k}},
$$

which collects how $h_{t-k}$ affects the next forget gate, input gate, and candidate. At the other endpoint,

$$
D_t^{\text{out}}
:=\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial c_t}
=\operatorname{Diag}\!\left(o_t\odot\left[1-\tanh^2(c_t)\right]\right)
$$

along the direct $c_t\rightarrow h_t$ edge. The contribution of this cell-state path to the long hidden-state Jacobian is therefore

$$
\boxed{
\left.\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-k}}\right|_{\text{cell path}}
=D_t^{\text{out}}
\left(F_tF_{t-1}\cdots F_{t-k+2}\right)
G_{t-k+1}.
}
$$

If the model learns $f_j\approx\mathbf 1$ over the relevant interval, then $F_j\approx I$ and the long middle product stays close to identity. The endpoint derivatives $G_{t-k+1}$ and $D_t^{\text{out}}$ can still attenuate the gradient, but they occur once each; they are not multiplied once per time step. This is how the additive cell path reduces the exponential dependence on distance.

<details className="doubt">
  <summary>Is the boxed expression the full derivative $\partial h_t/\partial h_{t-k}$?</summary>
  <p>No. It is one useful path inside the full computation graph. Because every gate also depends on earlier hidden states, the full Jacobian contains additional paths. The point is that the LSTM guarantees a trainable additive route whose repeated factors can remain near identity.</p>
</details>

### GRU: an additive carry path without a separate cell

A GRU compresses the same principle into one state:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_t &= \sigma(W_zx_t+U_zh_{t-1}+b_z),\\
r_t &= \sigma(W_rx_t+U_rh_{t-1}+b_r),\\
\widetilde h_t &= \tanh\!\left(W_hx_t+U_h(r_t\odot h_{t-1})+b_h\right),\\
h_t &= (1-z_t)\odot h_{t-1}+z_t\odot\widetilde h_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

<figure className="source-figure">
  <img src="/blog/from-recurrent-memory-to-attention/gru-cell-d2l.svg" alt="GRU computation graph with reset and update gates" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 5.</strong> A GRU uses a reset gate for constructing the candidate and an update gate for mixing the candidate with the old state. Source: <a href="https://d2l.ai/chapter_recurrent-modern/gru.html">Dive into Deep Learning</a>.</figcaption>
</figure>

Along the direct carry edge,

$$
\left.\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-1}}\right|_{\text{carry}}
=\operatorname{Diag}(1-z_t).
$$

When $z_t\approx0$, the GRU copies the previous state and the direct derivative is near identity. As with the LSTM, this is a protected path within the full Jacobian—not a claim that every gradient path has derivative one.

LSTMs and GRUs therefore address long-range gradient transport. They do **not** address the other RNN limitation: every forward and backward step is still sequential.

## 5. The encoder-decoder bottleneck

In a recurrent sequence-to-sequence model, an encoder processes the source states

$$
h_t^{\mathrm{enc}}
=f_{\mathrm{enc}}(x_t,h_{t-1}^{\mathrm{enc}}),
$$

and a decoder generates one target token at a time. The original fixed-context design compresses the complete source into the final encoder state:

$$
c=h_T^{\mathrm{enc}},
\qquad
s_m=g\!\left(s_{m-1},E(y_{m-1}),c\right).
$$

  <img src="/blog/from-recurrent-memory-to-attention/seq2seq-model-lecture.png" alt="Lecture slide showing a recurrent encoder-decoder sequence-to-sequence model" loading="lazy" />

</figure>

The same vector $c$ must preserve every source detail and serve every decoder step. Gating may help information reach $h_T^{\mathrm{enc}}$, but it does not remove this fixed-vector bottleneck.

<figure className="source-figure">
  <img src="/blog/from-recurrent-memory-to-attention/seq2seq-bottleneck-lecture.png" alt="Lecture slide showing the final encoder state being passed to every decoder step" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 7.</strong> The fixed-context bottleneck: the final encoder representation is the decoder's only view of the source. From Prof. Prathosh A. P.'s sequence-model lecture.</figcaption>
</figure>

The natural question is: why should the decoder use the same summary while producing every output token?

## 6. Attention as dynamic retrieval

Keep all encoder states $h_1,\ldots,h_T$. At decoder step $m$, score how useful each encoder state is for the current prediction. Bahdanau-style additive attention uses

$$
e_{m,t}
=v_a^\top\tanh\!\left(W_ss_{m-1}+W_hh_t+b_a\right).
$$

Normalize these scores over source positions:

$$
\alpha_{m,t}
=\frac{\exp(e_{m,t})}{\sum_{j=1}^{T}\exp(e_{m,j})},
\qquad
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{m,t}=1.
$$

The decoder receives a step-specific context vector

$$
\boxed{
c_m=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{m,t}h_t,
}
$$

and updates as

$$
s_m=g\!\left(s_{m-1},E(y_{m-1}),c_m\right).
$$

For example, if the attention weights are

$$
(\alpha_{m,1},\alpha_{m,2},\alpha_{m,3},\alpha_{m,4})
=(0.03,0.08,0.13,0.76),
$$

then $c_m$ is dominated by $h_4$, while still remaining a differentiable weighted combination of all encoder states.

  <figcaption><strong>Figure 8.</strong> At each decoder step, attention constructs a new weighted summary of the encoder states. From Prof. Prathosh A. P.'s sequence-model lecture.</figcaption>
</figure>

The matrix $[\alpha_{m,t}]$ can be visualized as a soft alignment between target and source positions.

<figure className="source-figure">
  <img src="/blog/from-recurrent-memory-to-attention/attention-map-lecture.png" alt="Heatmap of learned attention alignments between source and target words" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 9.</strong> Attention weights often expose a soft source-target alignment. From Prof. Prathosh A. P.'s sequence-model lecture.</figcaption>
</figure>

Attention changes two things:

1. **No single-vector compression:** each decoder step can retrieve different source information.
2. **A shorter gradient route:** the loss at decoder step $m$ connects directly to every encoder state through $c_m$ and $\alpha_{m,t}$, rather than only through $h_T^{\mathrm{enc}}$.

But this model is still recurrent: encoder states and decoder states are computed sequentially. Later, *Attention Is All You Need* showed that attention could become the main sequence-processing mechanism rather than an addition to recurrence. That is where this article stops.

## Quick summary

- A vanilla RNN transports information and gradients through repeated state transitions.
- BPTT exposes the long product $J_tJ_{t-1}\cdots J_{t-k+1}$.
- Products of recurrent Jacobians can vanish or explode exponentially with distance.
- LSTMs and GRUs add gated carry paths whose repeated derivatives can stay near identity.
- Classical encoder-decoder attention removes the fixed-context bottleneck by retrieving a new weighted source summary at every decoder step.
- Attention initially improved recurrent models; removing recurrence is the next story, not this one.
